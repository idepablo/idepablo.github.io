I recently built a portfolio project in Tableau with a fun premise: pretend I am an analyst for an NBA team, and the front office keeps asking questions. Which positions shoot best? Where is the assist market? How is our roster aging? I used fresh 2025-26 data and picked my team, the Boston Celtics, as the point of view. Working on a team I actually care about turned out to matter more than I expected.

The story walks through the analysis in six steps, and the League Drill Down sheet lets you expand from conference all the way down to individual players. The full interactive workbook is embedded at the end of this page, or you can open it on Tableau Public ↗.

The data

Everything comes from Basketball-Reference (Sports Reference LLC): 2025-26 regular season player totals, playoff totals, league standings, and the Celtics game-by-game log. I cleaned the exports into four CSVs, connected them in Tableau with relationships instead of joins, and validated the numbers against the official Celtics team statistics page. Every figure reconciled to the decimal, for example Jaylen Brown at 28.7 points per game (2,038 points over 71 games).

Cleaning came first, and it was the most instructive part

Basketball-Reference gives traded players multiple rows: one per team plus a combined row labeled 2TM, 3TM, or even 4TM. If you sum points without handling that, every traded player counts twice. James Harden, for example, shows a combined row (557 assists) plus separate rows for his time with the Clippers and Cavaliers.

The fix depends on the question. For team views you want the per-team rows, so Harden's Boston-relevant question is "what did he produce for each team." For player views you want exactly one row per player. I built two calculated fields for this: a simple Row Type flag, and a Season Row filter that uses a level-of-detail expression to keep the combined row for traded players and the only row for everyone else. That second field ended up being my first real level-of-detail expression, learned because the problem demanded it.

I also found a quirk of the current data: traded players can carry different positions on different rows. Older seasons encoded this with hybrid labels like "PG-SG" inside a single row, but the site now publishes one position per team stint. CJ McCollum was listed as a shooting guard in Washington and a point guard in Atlanta. Five players had this conflict. The combined row carries the season-level position, so filtering on Season Row resolves it, but I only knew that because I checked.

The centerpiece: when AVG lies

The most valuable lesson in the whole project fits in one comparison. The first version of my 3-point efficiency table used AVG of the 3P% column by team and position. It told me that Lakers centers shot a perfect 1.000 from three.

The problem is that AVG treats every player equally. A player who went 1-for-1 counts the same as a player who took 500 attempts. The honest number is total makes divided by total attempts, which weights by volume:

SUM([3P]) / SUM([3PA])
Before: AVG of the 3P% column. Lakers centers at 1.000, Phoenix centers at .62, extreme cells everywhere.
After: SUM(3P) / SUM(3PA). Volume-weighted, and most of the extremes correct themselves.

Most of the extreme cells corrected themselves. Phoenix centers dropped from .62 to .27 once Mark Williams' single made three stopped outvoting Khaman Maluach's 21 attempts. San Antonio's small forwards fell from .54 to .38 once Julian Champagnie's 512 attempts got their proper weight.

But the Lakers cell stayed at 1.000, and that is my favorite finding in the project. Jaxson Hayes went 3-for-3, and no other Lakers center attempted a single three all season. The formula is doing exactly what it should. The number is arithmetically perfect and still needs a human next to it saying "based on three attempts." I added attempt volume to the tooltip so the table documents its own reliability.

What the data says about Boston

Points vs. assists across the league, Celtics highlighted. The elite creators cluster far up and to the right.

A few findings from the Celtics angle:

  • Boston's shooting is position-agnostic. Every position shot between .35 and .38 from three, the flattest profile in the league. Most contenders concentrate their shooting in one or two spots.
  • Boston is a prime-age team with almost no youth production. Only 10% of the team's points came from players under 25, second lowest in the league behind New York. 75% came from the 25-29 band. For contrast, Washington got 77% of its scoring from under-25 players.
  • There is no elite playmaker. Derrick White led the team with 414 assists, followed closely by Payton Pritchard at 408. The league's top creators sit between 530 and 697, and the biggest single producer in the assist market is not even a guard: Nikola Jokić, a center, led the NBA with 697.

And the season arc itself: Boston opened 0-3, climbed steadily, peaked with a 9-2 February, and finished 56-26, second in the East. Almost all of it without Jayson Tatum, who played only 16 regular season games but averaged 21.8 points in them.

The season arc: 0-3 start, a 9-2 February, a 56-26 finish.

One league-wide anomaly I could not stop looking at: Detroit won 60 games while getting roughly 60% of its points from under-25 players. Every other team with that kind of youth share was in the lottery.

A note on totals versus per-game

All rankings in the workbook use season totals, which measure production plus availability. Per-game rankings differ slightly. Anthony Edwards edges Jaylen Brown per game, 28.8 to 28.7, but Brown's 71 games put him well ahead in totals. Both lenses are valid; they answer different questions. I noted the basis on each chart so nobody cross-checking against per-game leaderboards thinks something is wrong.

Tools and takeaways

Built in Tableau Public with four related data sources, calculated fields for data cleaning, a drill-down hierarchy from conference to player, and a story for presentation. The two habits I am taking with me: verify a cleaning step is actually needed before applying it (a commonly suggested fix for hybrid position labels does nothing on current data), and never trust an average of a ratio column.
